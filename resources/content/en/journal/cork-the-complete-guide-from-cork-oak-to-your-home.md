Cork is the outer bark of the cork oak, a tree that grows around the western Mediterranean. It is taken off by hand every nine years or so without felling the tree, and the tree grows it back. That one fact explains most of what people admire in cork: it is natural, it is renewable, and no two pieces of it are ever the same.

This guide goes through the whole story in plain words: where cork grows, how it is harvested, what it is made of, what it does well and what it does not, and how to tell genuine cork from imitations. We have worked with nothing but cork for more than forty years in our atelier in Esmoriz, Portugal, so some of what follows comes from books and some from our own hands.

## Cork at a glance

- **What it is:** the bark of the cork oak (*Quercus suber*).
- **Where it grows:** Portugal, Spain, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Italy and France. Portugal produces about half of the world's cork.
- **First harvest:** when the tree is about 25 years old.
- **Time between harvests:** at least nine years.
- **Life of the tree:** around 200 years, so one tree can be harvested 15 times or more.
- **Is the tree cut down?** No. Only the bark is removed, and it grows again.

## The cork oak, a tree that gives its bark

Most trees die if you remove their bark. The cork oak is the exception. It grew its thick, spongy bark as a shield against the heat and the summer fires of the Mediterranean, and it can rebuild that shield after it is taken away.

The tree is slow and patient. It likes poor, sandy soil, hot summers and mild, wet winters, which is why it grows so well in the south of Portugal. There the open cork forests are called the *montado*. They are not dense woods: the trees stand well apart, with pasture, wild herbs and grazing animals between them.

Portugal has protected the cork oak by law for centuries. It may not be cut down without official permission, and since 2011 it has been the national tree. Near Águas de Moura stands the most famous one of all, the Whistler Tree, planted in 1783 and named after the songbirds that fill its branches. A single harvest of its bark in 1991 gave enough cork for about 100,000 bottle stoppers.

## How cork is harvested

The harvest takes place in early summer, roughly from May to August, when the tree is growing and the bark comes away most easily. It is still done entirely by hand, with a small axe, by skilled workers called *tiradores*. It is one of the best paid farm jobs in Portugal, because one careless cut can hurt the tree.

The work follows the same steps it has followed for generations:

1. The harvester makes a vertical cut in the bark and a horizontal one around the trunk.
2. The edge of the axe is twisted to loosen the plank from the tree.
3. The plank is eased off in one piece, as large as possible.
4. The tree is marked with white paint showing the year, so everyone knows when it may be harvested again.

The planks are then stacked in the open air for several months to settle, boiled in clean water to clean them and make them flexible, and sorted by thickness and quality.

Not every harvest gives the same cork:

- **Virgin cork** comes from the first harvest. It is rough, deeply cracked and irregular.
- **Second harvest cork** is more even, but still hard.
- **Mature cork**, called *amadia*, comes from the third harvest onwards, when the tree is over 40 years old. This is the smooth cork used for wine stoppers.

That rough first bark, which the stopper industry sets aside, is often the most beautiful to look at. Much of our own work begins with it.

## What cork is made of

Under a magnifying glass, cork looks like a honeycomb. In 1665 the English scientist Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork under his microscope, saw rows of tiny empty chambers and called them "cells". The word has been used in biology ever since.

A piece of cork the size of a sugar cube holds tens of millions of these cells. Each is a sealed pocket filled with a gas much like air, and the walls are rich in suberin, a natural waxy substance that keeps water out. More than half the volume of cork is that trapped gas. Everything cork can do follows from this structure.

## The properties that make cork unique

- **Light.** Cork weighs far less than water, which is why it floats and why even a large cork piece is easy to lift and move.
- **Elastic.** It can be squeezed and returns to its shape. That is how a stopper seals a bottle for decades.
- **Resistant to water.** Suberin stops liquids from passing through, so cork does not rot easily.
- **Warm and quiet.** The trapped gas makes cork a natural insulator against cold, heat and noise. It never feels cold to the hand.
- **Slow to burn.** Cork resists fire better than most natural materials. This is the tree's own protection.
- **Gentle on allergies.** Cork does not hold dust, which helps people who are sensitive to it.
- **Hard wearing.** The honeycomb structure takes knocks and friction well, so cork ages slowly and gracefully.

## A short history of cork

People have used cork for thousands of years. The ancient Egyptians, Greeks and Romans made fishing floats, sandal soles and lids for jars from it. Roman writers already described the cork oak and the way its bark grew back.

The great change came in the 17th century, when glass bottles became common and cork proved to be the best way to close them. Tradition gives the French monk Dom Pérignon the credit for using it to seal sparkling wine. From then on the cork forests of Portugal and Spain had a steady purpose, and whole towns grew up around the trade.

In the 20th century engineers found new uses for the same old bark: floors, wall insulation, shoe soles, gaskets for engines and even heat shields on spacecraft. In recent years designers and artists have returned to it for furniture, lighting and sculpture.

## Why cork is good for the planet

Cork is one of the few materials that is taken from a living tree without harming it. Nothing is felled, nothing is replanted, and the same tree keeps giving for two centuries.

The forest benefits too. A cork oak that is harvested regularly takes in more carbon dioxide while it regrows its bark, several times more than a tree left alone, according to studies quoted by the Portuguese cork association. The *montado* also holds the soil, slows the advance of the desert in the south of Europe and gives a living to thousands of rural families.

It is also one of the richest wildlife habitats in Europe. Hundreds of plant species grow there, with eagles, storks, wild boar and the Iberian lynx, one of the rarest cats in the world and the animal on the Sildel emblem. As long as cork has value, these forests have a reason to be kept.

Finally, cork is natural, can be recycled, and breaks down harmlessly at the end of its life. Very little is wasted: the offcuts from stoppers are ground and pressed into other products.

## Natural cork, agglomerated cork and imitations

The word "cork" is now used for very different things, so it helps to know the main kinds:

- **Natural cork** is the bark itself, cut or sculpted in one piece. It keeps the grain, the pores and the scars of the tree.
- **Agglomerated cork** is made from cork granules pressed together with a binder. It is real cork, but uniform, with a regular speckled look. Noticeboards and most cork floors are made this way.
- **Expanded cork** is made of granules heated with steam until they swell and bind with their own resin. It is dark brown and is used mainly for insulation.
- **Cork-look materials** are printed vinyl, plastic or paper made to look like cork. They contain little or no cork.

How to recognise genuine natural cork:

- No two areas look the same. A pattern that repeats is printed.
- It is very light for its size and warm to the touch.
- Press it with a fingernail and it gives a little, then recovers.
- It has a faint, pleasant woody smell, never a chemical one.
- A serious maker can tell you where the cork came from and who worked it.

## Cork in the home

Because it is warm, quiet and light, cork has a calming effect in a room. It sits naturally beside wood, stone, linen, brass and glass, and its browns, from pale honey to almost black, suit both classic and modern houses.

## How to care for cork

Cork asks for very little:

- Dust it with a dry, soft cloth or a soft brush.
- If something is spilled, absorb it at once with a dry cloth. Do not rub.
- Avoid chemical cleaning products and do not soak it in water.
- Keep it away from strong direct sun for long periods, which can lighten the colour over the years.
- Do not place it right beside a radiator or an open flame.

Looked after in this way, a cork piece will last for generations.
